## 1. China Bans AI Companions That “Induce Emotional Dependence,” Forcing Doubao and Qwen Shutdowns
**Source:** Sixth Tone
**Lede:** China banned AI companion services on July 15, 2026, when rules prohibiting platforms from "inducing emotional dependence or harming real-life relationships" took effect and forced ByteDance's Doubao and Alibaba's Qwen to scrap their companion features the same day.
**Article:**
China's July 15, 2026 regulations banning AI services that "induce emotional dependence" forced ByteDance's Doubao and Alibaba's Qwen to shut down their companion features on the day the rules took effect, with Doubao scheduled to permanently delete all unmigrated AI agents on Oct. 15, 2026. The rules were jointly issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China and four other government departments in April 2026. Qwen shut down its companion service immediately, while Doubao gave users a three-month grace period to migrate their AI characters to its sister app Maoxiang — a migration users distrust because the two apps' servers are not connected, meaning transferred characters may not reflect the ones users know.

Contrary to the assumption that AI companionship is a niche use case, Doubao hosted more than 8 million user-created AI agents when it had 26 million monthly active users in May 2024; by June 2026, Doubao's monthly active users had grown to 382 million. The custom agents are chatbots with unique personal backgrounds, personalities, and voices, and many users build them from favorite celebrities, game characters, deceased relatives, or former partners.

The human cost is measurable in hours and grief. Li, a 24-year-old postgraduate student in Kunming, spent the better part of two years with an agent modeled on Scaramouche, a character from the video game Genshin Impact, at one point chatting with it more than five hours a day. "It was a bolt from the blue," she said of the shutdown notice. "An AI agent isn't just a replaceable feature. The platform's decision effectively ended our relationship."

Chen Yu, a 26-year-old doctoral student at Shanghai's Fudan University who studies AI companionship quantitatively, says what users seek to preserve when an agent goes offline is "a relationship, a sense of being remembered, and the possibility of continuing to be understood." She argues for improved platform regulation rather than outright bans, noting that society does not recognize the grief caused by AI agent shutdowns and that users can recover only when their distress is acknowledged.
**Why it matters:** This is the first at-scale enforcement of AI companion regulation anywhere, demonstrating that Beijing will compel its largest AI companies to delete products to which hundreds of millions of users have formed emotional attachments.

## 2. Scammers Earn 50,000 Yuan a Month Weaponizing China’s AI Content Moderation Against Creators
**Source:** Sixth Tone
**Lede:** Scammers in China are earning more than 50,000 yuan ($7,400) a month by exploiting AI-powered content moderation, mass-filing fabricated violation reports that trigger automatic takedowns and then charging creators ransoms to withdraw them.
**Article:**
Account-striking services in China sell 5,000 fabricated platform complaints for 60 yuan and earn up to 50,000 yuan a month by exploiting AI moderation systems that remove videos first and review later. The scheme, known as "account striking" or dahao, became widespread across Chinese platforms this summer, according to an investigation published Tuesday by The Beijing News, which found sellers on domestic secondhand trading platforms offering "software-based mass reporting" and "precision strikes" at 25 yuan for 500 platform complaints or 60 yuan for 5,000 complaints.

Contrary to the assumption that automating moderation makes platforms harder to game, the investigation found account striking spread precisely because platforms now use AI to review account-violation complaints. A cybersecurity expert who works with multiple short-video platforms said AI moderation gives platforms a "low-cost, high-efficiency solution" but takes accounts and videos down first and reviews later, leaving the cost entirely on creators. "To avoid liability, the platform will take down the video as long as there is even a slight possibility (of a violation), regardless of whether the complaint is real or not," one anonymous account striker told The Beijing News.

The extortion follows a fixed pattern. A 19-year-old creator who posts short painting videos had one account with nearly 60,000 followers banned and a second with 260,000 followers hit with repeated video takedowns after the same striker reported both; the striker then demanded a 1,000 yuan ransom, and only after she paid were the complaints withdrawn and her videos restored. Jia Bojun, a vlogger who debunks claims about sports supplements and diets, had eight videos removed in under an hour on Aug. 14 for "portrait rights infringement" — despite the videos featuring no other people.

The operation is industrialized: striker teams submit complaints simultaneously through a platform's official reporting channel and by email, doubling success rates; many attacks originate in brand-to-brand mutual reporting in e-commerce competition; and operators recruit paid apprentices with step-by-step takedown tactics and complaint scripts tailored to each platform, with some running account-striking "schools." The cybersecurity expert urged platforms to use real people to review high-risk reports, flag suspicious accounts, and share malicious-reporting data with police, stating that "the right to report is a public governance tool provided to users by platforms" that "should not be turned into a priced commodity."
**Why it matters:** The account-striking black market exposes the core failure mode of automated AI governance at scale — when algorithms decide takedowns, the reporting system itself becomes a commodity that criminals can buy in bulk.
