## 1. NHTSA Opens Investigation Into Tesla's Cybercab Hours After Public Driverless Rides Begin in Austin
**Source:** Ars Technica
**Brief:** The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has opened an investigation — called an Audit Query — into Tesla's Cybercab, a two-seater electric robotaxi with no steering wheel or brake pedals, hours after hundreds of riders took driverless trips in downtown Austin. The probe examines the data and process Tesla used to determine that the Cybercab meets all existing federal safety standards without an exemption, even though Amazon's Zoox won the first such exemption this summer, capped at 2,500 vehicles in 2026. NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison said the agency supports automated vehicles, "but as the federal regulator, we need to ensure that all of our laws are followed." A Texas state database lists 45 Cybercabs in Tesla's in-state fleet, and Elon Musk has said Tesla intends to deploy millions of the vehicles annually.
**Bottom line:** The Audit Query can force Tesla to change the Cybercab or its process and can lead to civil penalties — Volvo paid $130 million in 2023 after a similar recall-process investigation.

## 3. Tesla's FSD v14.3.9 Can Take Control From Drivers to Evade Collisions
**Source:** Teslarati
**Brief:** Tesla has begun rolling out Full Self-Driving (Supervised) v14.3.9, which can activate itself even while the driver is operating the car manually. The new active safety layer engages when it detects an imminent collision that Automatic Emergency Braking alone may not prevent, heavy driver distraction, or an accidental FSD disengagement. Unlike conventional AEB, which brakes mainly in a straight line, the system can combine steering, braking, and acceleration to reach a safer path — changing lanes or moving to a shoulder — and then continue driving rather than stopping. The feature requires FSD to be enabled plus an active FSD purchase or subscription, does not make the vehicle unsupervised, and Tesla has not published expected intervention rates or how it handles false positives.
**Bottom line:** FSD is no longer only a feature drivers turn on — in the final seconds before a crash, it can now activate on its own and execute a coordinated evasive maneuver.
**Link:** https://www.teslarati.com/tesla-full-self-driving-overtake-manual-driving-avoid-disaster/

## 4. Michigan Hyundai Dealer Billed an EV Driver $418 for One 40-Minute Charging Session
**Source:** Electrek
**Brief:** Elder Hyundai's Macomb Township, Michigan store charged a driver $418 for a single EV charging session after the charger appeared as publicly available on the EVgo app. Stanley Kpenkann of Clinton Township, Michigan, driving a rented Chevy Bolt EV, discovered the charge only after a roughly 40-minute session; the dealer's rate is $5 per kWh plus $5 for every minute connected. The case follows a June report of a Hyundai dealer in Union, New Jersey charging $15 per kWh to top off an IONIQ 5, and both reflect a structural gap: unlike gas stations, EV charge point operators in many states are not required to advertise prices on signage visible from the street before a driver plugs in. Electrek is calling for regulation requiring for-profit charging hosts, including dealerships, to exceed baseline standards to qualify for public incentive dollars.
**Bottom line:** Dealership chargers listed as "public" can impose extreme per-kWh and per-minute pricing because most states do not require EV charging price disclosure before a driver plugs in.
**Link:** https://electrek.co/2026/09/05/when-public-charging-stations-arent-so-public-and-why-it-matters/
